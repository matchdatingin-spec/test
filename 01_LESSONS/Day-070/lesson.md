Now process talk orientation. A good process explanation answers six listener questions naturally. What is the goal? What do we need? What happens first? Which actions overlap or wait? What problem or choice matters? How do we know the process is complete?

Again, this is not a spoken checklist. It is listener logic. Your talk should quietly answer these questions.

Choose a process you genuinely understand. It can be preparing a room for a meeting, making a simple drink or meal, packing for a day trip, organising a desk, submitting a form at an office, preparing for an online class, caring for a small group of plants, or another safe familiar process.

Do not choose a process that requires technical safety instructions or specialised knowledge. The checkpoint tests explanation skill, not expert knowledge.

Today one model process will be preparing a small room for a community reading session. Listen first for the overall structure, not exact wording.

The goal is to prepare a quiet and welcoming room for a one-hour reading session. I start by checking the number of visitors because that number decides how many chairs and books we need. The room near the entrance is larger, but it is often noisy, so I usually choose the smaller room at the back. It is quiet enough for twelve people, and it has a window that gives the room good natural light.

Before I move any furniture, I open the window and check the floor. Then I place a long table near the wall and arrange the chairs in a half-circle. I use the light chairs because they are easier to move than the wooden chairs. The wooden ones are too heavy for this short session. While another volunteer wipes the table, I bring the books from the cupboard where we keep the reading materials.

After the chairs are ready, I place the books on the table with their covers facing up. I keep a few simple books at the front because new readers need an easy choice. The longer books go at the back, and the largest book stays in the centre so everyone can see it. I also place enough water near the door for the volunteers.

I keep the window open until the room feels fresh. As soon as the readers arrive, I welcome them and show them where to sit. Before the session begins, I check that every reader has a book and that the walking space is clear. The room is ready when the books are easy to reach, the chairs are comfortable, and everyone can hear the reader clearly.

A two-to-three-minute talk needs pace, not filler. Speak in thought groups. Give listener a small pause when one stage ends. Do not race because you want to fit everything. Do not stretch simple steps with repeated empty phrases.

Checkpoint scoring. First, orientation. Did first two sentences make topic and goal clear?

Second, sequence. Could listener place the stages in correct order? Did before and after point correctly? Did while show actual overlap? Did until have an endpoint? Did as soon as show immediate action?

Third, connection. Did and add, but contrast, or offer a choice, so give a result, and because give a reason? Did you avoid double because and so?

Fourth, identification. Did who follow a person, which or that follow a thing, and where follow a place? Did you keep English noun-first order? Did you remove extra he, she, it, or there where relative word already did the job?

Fifth, evaluation. Did comparative form compare two clearly? Did superlative choose the top item without double marking? Did too show an unsuitable excess and enough show suitability?

Sixth, long-answer flow. Did you give explanations and concrete details instead of fragments? Did every detail support the process? Did you avoid translating silently for long periods?

Seventh, completion. Did ending tell listener how the process becomes ready or finished?

Eighth, delivery. Was the talk understandable, paced in meaning groups, and resilient after small mistakes?
