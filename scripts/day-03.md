# Day 03 — Did You Go or Did You Went? ഈ Confusion ഇനി വേണ്ട

**Target length:** 12–14 minutes
**Level:** A2 / B1
**One-line promise:** One rule — "only one word carries the past" — and this mistake disappears from your English forever.

## 🎬 1. THE HOOK (0:00 – 1:00)

*(Fast, playful. Almost like catching someone.)*

> *"Did you went to Kochi?"*
> *"Did you go to Kochi?"*

*(Pause 2 seconds.)*

If you hesitated even for one second — this video is for you.

And here is the strange part. You already **know** the past tense of *go* is *went*. You learned it in Class 4. Go, went, gone. You can say it in your sleep.

So why do you still get this wrong?

Because nobody told you **why**. They only told you to memorise. And memory fails when you are speaking fast, when you are nervous, when the interviewer is staring at you.

Today, no memorising. Just one piece of logic. And after that, *"Did you went"* will actually sound wrong to your ears — which is the real goal. Let's go.

## 🧠 2. THE CORE LOGIC (1:00 – 3:00)

Here is the rule. Write it down.

### 👉 In one English verb group, only ONE word carries the past.

One. Not two. If two words in the **same** verb group show the past, the sentence breaks.

*(Important — be precise here.)* ⚠️ A long sentence **can** have several past verbs, because it has several verb groups: ✅ *He **said** he **was** tired.* ✅ Both are past, and both are correct. The rule is about **one** verb group — one *did* + one verb working together.

Look at the simple statement:

> ✅ **I went to Kochi.**

Who is carrying the past here? **Went.** Only one word. Perfect.

Now we make it a question. And English does something interesting — it brings in a helper word: **did**.

And *did* is already the past form of *do*. **Did** is a past word. It is already carrying the load.

So now, if you also say *went*, you have **two** past words:

> ❌ **Did** you **went**? → past + past = ❌

Two people are carrying the same one box. English doesn't allow it.

### The Boss and the Worker

Let me give you a picture that will stay in your head forever.

Think of **did** as the **boss**, and the main verb as the **worker**.

When the boss walks in, the boss takes responsibility for the **time**. And the worker becomes free — the worker goes back to his simple, plain, basic form.

- Boss absent → worker carries the past → **I went.**
- Boss arrives → boss carries the past, worker relaxes → **Did I go?**

That's it. That's the whole lesson.

**Whenever *did* is the helper, its main verb must be plain.** Go, not went. Eat, not ate. See, not saw. Buy, not bought.

*(Say together.)* Did you **go**. Did you **eat**. Did you **see**. Did you **buy**.

And the beautiful part? This same boss rule works for negatives too:

- ❌ I didn't went → ✅ **I didn't go.**
- ❌ He didn't came → ✅ **He didn't come.**

One rule. Questions and negatives. Both fixed.

## 🔍 3. SENTENCE BREAKDOWN (3:00 – 8:00)

### Sentence 1 — built piece by piece

**Malayalam:** നീ ഇന്നലെ ഓഫീസിൽ പോയോ?

Watch the Malayalam. Where is the question? It is hiding in the **ending** — **പോയോ**. The little **-ഓ** at the end turns it into a question. The word order does not change at all.

**പോയി** (statement) → **പോയോ** (question). Same order, one sound added.

This is why Malayalis ask questions like ❌ *"You went office yesterday?"* — because in your language, you just change the tone at the end. You never move any word.

But English **moves** words. English builds a question in a fixed frame:

**[Helper] + [Person] + [Plain verb] + [Rest]**

Let's build it:

1. **Helper (carries the past)** → Did
2. **Person** → you
3. **Plain verb** (boss is here, so plain!) → go
4. **Where** → to the office
5. **When** → yesterday

> ✅ **"Did you go to the office yesterday?"**

*(Point out.)* Look at position one. The **helper is standing at the front door.** In English, the question is announced at the *beginning*. In Malayalam, the question is revealed at the *end*.

That is the single biggest reason Malayalis sound unclear in English questions. You are announcing your question too late.

### Sentence 2 — built piece by piece

**Malayalam:** ഞാൻ ഇന്നലെ ചോറ് കഴിച്ചില്ല.

Now a negative. Your brain sees കഴിച്ചില്ല — that is past + no. So it produces: ❌ *"I didn't ate."* Both words screaming "past!"

Apply the boss rule:

1. **Person** → I
2. **Helper + not** → did not / didn't ← *the past lives here*
3. **Plain verb** → eat ← *free man, no past*
4. **What** → lunch

> ✅ **"I didn't eat lunch yesterday."**

*(Tip.)* Also — don't say "I didn't eat rice" when you mean lunch. In English, meals have names: **breakfast, lunch, dinner.** ചോറ് കഴിച്ചോ? is not *"Did you eat rice?"* It is **"Have you eaten?"** or **"Did you have lunch?"**

### Five more practical examples

*(Show ❌ then ✅. Get the audience to say the ✅ out loud.)*

| Malayalam | ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|---|
| നീ അവനെ കണ്ടോ? | Did you saw him? | **Did you see him?** |
| അവൾ എന്ത് പറഞ്ഞു? | What she said? | **What did she say?** |
| നിങ്ങൾ ബസ്സ് കിട്ടിയോ? | Did you got the bus? | **Did you catch the bus?** |
| ഞാൻ അത് ചെയ്തില്ല | I didn't did it | **I didn't do it.** |
| എപ്പോൾ വന്നു? | When you came? | **When did you come?** |

*(Stop at row 2 and row 5.)* These two are special. Look — **what**, **when**, **where**, **why**, **how**. Even when you start with a question word, the helper still has to come:

- ❌ What she said? → ✅ **What did she say?**
- ❌ Where you went? → ✅ **Where did you go?**
- ❌ Why he called? → ✅ **Why did he call?**
- ❌ How you reached? → ✅ **How did you get here?**

The pattern: **Question word + did + person + plain verb.**

*(Chant it.)* What – did – you – say. Where – did – you – go. Why – did – he – call.

### The exception you must know

Among **simple-tense** questions like these, there's one case where you don't need *did*, and Malayalis get it wrong in the opposite direction.

*(Quick note.)* ✅ *Did* is also not needed when the sentence **already has** a helper: ✅ *Was he there?* · *Can she swim?* · *Has he left?* ✅ Those already have their own boss, so they never hire *did*.

When the question word is asking about **the subject** — the doer itself — no helper is needed.

- ✅ **Who called you?** *(not "Who did called you")*
- ✅ **What happened?** *(not "What did happened")*
- ✅ **Who told you that?**

Why? Because there is no gap in the middle to fill. *Who* is already sitting in the subject's chair. The verb keeps the past: **called, happened, told.**

Compare and feel the difference:
- **Who** called you? → asking about the doer → no *did*
- **Who did** you call? → asking about the receiver → *did* needed

*(Smile.)* Beautiful, right? Two tiny sentences, completely different meanings.

## ⚠️ 4. THE MALAYALI TRAP (8:00 – 10:00)

Here is the deepest trap, and almost no teacher explains it.

In Malayalam, **the verb ending carries everything at once**. Tense, question, negative — all packed into the tail of one word.

Look:

- പോകുന്നു → present
- പോയി → past
- പോയോ? → past + question
- പോയില്ല → past + negative
- പോയില്ലേ? → past + negative + question

*(Amazed tone.)* Five different meanings and the word order never changed **once**. Malayalam is a genius language for this. Everything happens inside the verb.

English works differently. English marks **some** things on the verb — *go/went, walk/walked* — but for questions and negatives it prefers to **hire a helper** and line the words up in order:

| Meaning | Malayalam | English |
|---|---|---|
| statement | പോയി | I **went** |
| question | പോയോ? | **Did** I **go**? |
| negative | പോയില്ല | I **didn't go** |
| negative question | പോയില്ലേ? | **Didn't** I **go**? |

So the trap is this: **your brain tries to change the verb, when English wants you to change the order.**

That is why we say *"Did you went?"* — you changed the verb (correctly, by Malayalam instinct) *and* added the helper (correctly, by English rule). You did both jobs. You worked twice as hard and got it wrong. 😄

**The fix — a 2-second mental check before every past question:**

> *"Did I already say* did *? Then the next verb must be a baby verb — plain and simple."*

Practice these three out loud with me, fast:

1. Did you finish it? ✅
2. He didn't tell me. ✅
3. When did she leave? ✅

Feel how *finish, tell, leave* all stay plain. That plainness is the sound of correct English.
