# Day 09 — May vs Might: Which One Shows More Possibility?

**Target length:** 11–13 minutes
**Level:** B1
**One-line promise:** *May* = a fair chance. *Might* = a smaller chance. And in real speech, one of them is far more useful than the other.

---

## 🎬 1. THE HOOK (0:00 – 1:00)

*(Curious, conversational.)*

Your friend asks: *"Are you coming to the party tonight?"*

And you want to say **ഞാൻ വരും എന്ന് തോന്നുന്നു... ഒരുപക്ഷേ.** So you say:

> ❌ *"May be I will come."*

*(Stop. Look at camera.)*

The problem is in the first two words. *"May be"* written as **two** words is a helper + verb — a completely different thing from the one-word comment **maybe**. And once you've written *may*, you can't add *will* after it: English does not allow **two modal helpers** *(may, might, will, can, could, should, must)* side by side in the same verb group.

The natural sentences are:
> ✅ **"I might come."**
> ✅ **"Maybe I'll come."**
> ✅ **"I may come, I'm not sure yet."**

*(Warm.)*

Have you said "may be I will come"? Almost every Malayali has. Today we fix it — and I'll show you exactly how much possibility each word carries, so you can control the meaning like a dial. Let's go.

## 🧠 2. THE CORE LOGIC (1:00 – 3:00)

Think of a **percentage dial** for "maybe."

| Word | Rough feeling | Feeling |
|---|---|---|
| **will** | confident | It's happening. |
| **may** | a real, fair chance | Could easily go either way. |
| **might** | a bit more doubtful | Possible, but I doubt it. |
| **won't** | ruled out | Not happening. |

*(Say this out loud.)* ⚠️ These are **feelings, not percentages** — context and tone change them completely. Treat the table as a dial you can feel, not a number you can measure. ✅

*(Honest teaching.)* Now listen carefully, because this is important: the difference between *may* and *might* is **very small**. Native speakers often use them interchangeably. Nobody will misunderstand you if you swap them.

So don't stress about 50% vs 30%. Instead, learn these **two solid, practical differences** — these actually matter:

### Difference 1: For PERMISSION, *may* is the normal choice.

- ✅ *May I come in?* = asking permission ← formal, polite
- ⚠️ *Might I come in?* ← also real English, but **markedly** formal and old-fashioned 😄

**So: for permission, use MAY.** *May I...?* is the formal, respectful way to ask.

### Difference 2: For imaginary or very unlikely things, use MIGHT.

- ✅ *If I won the lottery, I might buy an island.* ← imaginary → *might*
- ✅ *He might have missed the bus.* ← guessing about the past

**So: unreal, doubtful, guessing → MIGHT.**

### The practical summary

*(Say strongly.)*

> **Asking permission → MAY I.**
> **Guessing or unsure → MIGHT.**
> **Both work for "maybe" — MIGHT is safer and more common in everyday speech.**

If you remember nothing else: when you're unsure about something, say **might**. It works almost everywhere.

## 🔍 3. SENTENCE BREAKDOWN (3:00 – 8:00)

### Sentence 1 — built piece by piece

**Malayalam:** ഒരുപക്ഷേ ഇന്ന് മഴ പെയ്യും.

Your brain: ❌ *"May be today rain will come."*

Let's take that apart. Two problems:
1. *may be* (two words) → here you need **maybe** (one word), or a restructured sentence
2. *rain will come* → English says **it will rain** *(rain is a verb!)*

*(Important — don't over-correct!)* ✅ **maybe + will is perfectly fine.** *"Maybe it will rain"* is natural, correct English — *maybe* comments on the whole sentence, and *will* makes the prediction. The problem in the original is the **two-word *may be* plus *will***, not the word *maybe* itself. ⭐

Now, two correct ways to build it.

**Option A — using *maybe* (an adverb, goes at the front):**
1. **maybe** → Maybe
2. **Who/What** → it
3. **will** → will
4. **Verb** → rain
5. **When** → today

> ✅ **"Maybe it will rain today."**

**Option B — using *might* (a helper, goes in the middle):**
1. **What** → It
2. **Helper** → might
3. **Plain verb** → rain
4. **When** → today

> ✅ **"It might rain today."**

*(Key structural insight — emphasise this.)*

**maybe** and **might** sit in **different positions**:
- **maybe** = a comment word → goes at the **beginning** of the sentence
- **might** = a helper verb → goes **inside**, before the main verb

- ✅ *Maybe he is busy.* / ✅ *He might be busy.*
- ❌ *He maybe busy.* ← wrong! there's no verb at all here
- ❌ *Might he is busy.* ← wrong! that's a question shape

*(Warning about "may be" as two words.)* There **is** a correct use of *may be* as two words — when *may* is the helper and *be* is the verb:

- ✅ *He **may be** at home.* = perhaps he is at home ← two words, helper + be
- ✅ **Maybe** he is at home. = same meaning ← one word, comment

Both correct! But ❌ *"May be I will come"* is wrong because you used the two-word helper version **and then** added *will* — two modals in a row.

### Sentence 2 — built piece by piece

**Malayalam:** സാർ, ഞാൻ അകത്ത് വരാമോ?

Look at **വരാമോ** — the polite request tail again. And again, notice: politeness is inside the verb in Malayalam, but at the **front** in English.

Which word? This is **permission** → so **MAY**.

1. **Permission helper** → May
2. **Who** → I
3. **Plain verb** → come
4. **Where** → in

> ✅ **"May I come in?"**

*(Politeness ladder for permission.)*

| Sentence | Level | Where to use |
|---|---|---|
| *Can I come in?* | casual | friends, colleagues |
| *Could I come in?* | polite | most situations |
| **May I come in?** | formal | teacher, boss, interview, elders |

*(Interview tip.)* At an interview door, say **"May I come in, sir?"** or **"Good morning, may I come in?"** That single sentence sets a respectful tone before you even sit down.

More *May I* phrases worth memorising:
- ✅ *May I know your name?*
- ✅ *May I ask you something?*
- ✅ *May I use your phone?*
- ✅ *May I leave early today?*

### Five more real-life examples

| Situation | ✅ Correct | Which word & why |
|---|---|---|
| Boss asks if you'll finish today | **I might finish it today, but I'm not sure.** | *might* = honest uncertainty |
| Doctor warning you | **This medicine may cause sleepiness.** | *may* = formal/written possibility |
| Friend is late | **He might have missed the train.** | *might have* = guessing about the past |
| Asking permission in an exam hall | **May I go to the washroom?** | *may* = permission |
| Planning your weekend | **We might go to Wayanad this weekend.** | *might* = plan not fixed |

*(Highlight row 3.)* **might have + V3** is such a useful pattern for guessing about the past:
- ✅ *She might have forgotten.*
- ✅ *They might have left already.*
- ✅ *I might have left my keys at home.*

*(Highlight row 2.)* Notice — in **formal writing** (medicine labels, notices, contracts), English prefers **may**:
- ✅ *Students may collect their certificates on Monday.*
- ✅ *Prices may change without notice.*

## ⚠️ 4. THE MALAYALI TRAP (8:00 – 10:00)

### Trap 1: The "may be I will" double

We covered it, but let's understand **why** it happens.

In Malayalam: **ഒരുപക്ഷേ ഞാൻ വരും.** Here **ഒരുപക്ഷേ** = maybe, and **വരും** = will come. Malayalam happily keeps **both** — the uncertainty word *and* the future ending. There's no conflict.

English can't copy that shape **directly**, because *may* and *will* are both helpers, and English allows only **one** helper of that kind in a verb group:

| Malayalam | ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|---|
| ഒരുപക്ഷേ ഞാൻ വരും | May be I will come | **I might come.** / **Maybe I'll come.** |
| ഒരുപക്ഷേ അവൻ വരില്ല | May be he will not come | **He might not come.** |
| ഒരുപക്ഷേ ശരിയാകും | May be it will be correct | **It might work.** |

**Rule: only one modal per verb group.** ❌ *may will* is impossible. ✅ Use **might/may** alone, or the one-word comment **maybe** with *will*.

*(Precision — so you're not misled later.)* ⚠️ This limit applies to **modals only**. Other helpers stack quite happily: ✅ *He **may have been** waiting.* ✅ *It **will have been** finished.* ⭐ One modal, then as many of *have/be* as the meaning needs. ✅

*(Very important — don't get this backwards!)* ⭐ **"Maybe I'll come" is 100% correct.** ✅ The word *maybe* is not a helper, so it sits happily with *will*. ✅ What breaks is only ❌ *may* + *will* — two helpers. ⭐

### Trap 2: "may not" vs "might not" vs "cannot"

Malayalam **ഇല്ല / പറ്റില്ല** covers a lot of ground, so these get mixed:

- ✅ *He **might not** come.* = perhaps he won't come *(uncertainty)*
- ✅ *You **may not** enter.* = you are **not allowed** *(permission refused!)*
- ✅ *He **can't** come.* = it's impossible for him *(no ability)*

*(Warning.)* This is a real danger. **"You may not"** is a **prohibition**, not a maybe! If you say ⚠️ *"He may not come"* meaning "perhaps he won't," a formal listener might hear *"he is forbidden to come."*

**Safe practice: for uncertainty, always use MIGHT NOT.** Leave *may not* for rules and prohibitions.

### Trap 3: Forgetting the invisible "be" (again!)

Same old friend from Day 04 and Day 07:

- ❌ *He might busy* → ✅ **He might be busy.**
- ❌ *It may difficult* → ✅ **It may be difficult.**
- ❌ *She might at home* → ✅ **She might be at home.**

*(Chant it.)* **might be. may be. might be.** After a helper, when there's no action, insert **be**.

### Bonus: better ways to sound unsure

*(Give the audience an upgrade.)* Instead of always saying "maybe," try these natural phrases:

- ✅ *I'm not sure, but I think he's coming.*
- ✅ *It's possible.*
- ✅ *Probably.* / *Probably not.*
- ✅ *There's a chance.*
- ✅ *Let's see.*
- ✅ *I'll let you know.*

*(Smile.)* That last one — **"I'll let you know"** — is the most useful sentence in this entire lesson. Use it whenever you can't commit.
