# Day 43 — Why Native English Sounds Fast: Reductions and Linking Explained

**Target length:** 14–16 minutes
**Level:** B1
**One-line promise:** Natives aren't speaking fast — they're **joining** and **shrinking** words. Learn the patterns, and your ears slow them down.

Today I teach you the patterns. Your listening will improve **immediately** — not in months. Today. Let's go. 🚀

## 🧠 2. THE CORE LOGIC (1:00 – 4:00)

*(Slow. This concept is genuinely eye-opening.)*

### Why Malayalam ears struggle specifically 👂

*(This is the key insight — explain it properly.)*

Malayalam is a **syllable-timed** language. Every syllable gets roughly **equal time and equal weight**:

**മ-ല-യാ-ള-ത്തിൽ** → ma-la-yaa-la-thil ✅ Each piece is clear. Each piece is pronounced. 🥁

*(Emphasise.)* That's why Malayalam sounds **rhythmic and even** — like a steady drum. And it's why Malayalam is **easy to read aloud**: what you see is what you say. ✅

**English leans the other way.** English is much more **stress-timed**. Some syllables get **big time**, and the rest get **crushed**:

**"I want to go to the market."**
→ *"I **WANT** to go to the **MAR**ket."*
→ Actually spoken: **"I wanna go tuh the MARket."** 🧽

*(Point out.)* The important words got **full time**. The small words — *to, the* — got **squeezed to almost nothing**. ✅

*(The consequence for you.)*

> ### 👉 Your ears expect every syllable to be pronounced clearly. English squeezes and swallows many of them.

*(That's the whole problem.)* You're listening for sounds that **aren't there**. ✅

### The 3 things English does to words

*(Write these — this is the framework.)*

**1️⃣ REDUCTION** — words get shorter 🗜️
*want to* → **wanna** · *going to* → **gonna** · *got to* → **gotta**

**2️⃣ LINKING** — words join together 🔗
*an apple* → **anapple** · *pick it up* → **pickitup**

**3️⃣ DELETION** — sounds disappear 🕳️
*next day* → **nexday** · *and* → **'n**

*(Slogan.)*
> **Reduce. Link. Delete.** 🗜️🔗🕳️

### Two important things before we start ⚠️

*(Manage expectations honestly.)*

**Point 1: You must LEARN these to HEAR them.** 👂

*(Explain.)* Your brain can only hear sounds it recognises. Once you learn that *"wanna"* = *"want to,"* you'll suddenly hear it **everywhere** — in songs, in movies, in every video. It was always there. Your brain just filtered it out. ✅

**Point 2: You do NOT have to SPEAK this way.** 🗣️

*(Very important — say this clearly.)* This lesson is mainly for **listening**. You don't need to fake an American accent. Speaking clearly with an Indian accent is **perfectly professional** — and internationally, Indian English is one of the most widely spoken varieties. 🇮🇳

*(But.)* Learning **a few** reductions will make you sound more natural and — more importantly — help you **understand** everyone. ✅

*(Slogan.)*
> **Learn them to HEAR. Use a few to sound natural. Never fake an accent.** ✅

## 🔍 3. SENTENCE BREAKDOWN (4:00 – 12:00)

### Sentence 1 — decoded piece by piece

**What you hear:** *"Whaddaya gonna do about it?"* 🤯

*(Decode it slowly, piece by piece, on screen.)*

**Piece 1 — "Whaddaya"**
- *What* → the final **t** becomes a soft **d** *(a very American thing)* → "wha**d**"
- *are you* → squeezed into **"aya"** or **"ya"**
- → **What are you** = *"Whaddaya"* ✅

*(Important — this is why decoding is hard!)* ⚠️ *"Whaddaya"* is **ambiguous**. It can come from **either** *what are you* **or** *what do you* — both reduce to the same sound. ✅ You tell them apart from **what follows**:
- *"Whaddaya **gonna** do?"* → *gonna* = *going to*, so it's **what ARE you** ✅
- *"Whaddaya **wanna** do?"* → *wanna* = *want to*, so it's **what DO you** ✅

**Piece 2 — "gonna"**
- *going to* → **gonna** ✅ *(the most common reduction in English!)*

**Piece 3 — "do about it"**
- *about it* → the **t** of "about" joins to "it" → **"abou-dit"** 🔗
- → *"do abou-dit"* ✅

**Full decode:**
> **"What are you going to do about it?"** → *"Whaddaya gonna do abou-dit?"* ✅

*(Don't mix these two up!)* ⚠️ **gonna = going to** ✅ · **wanna = want to** ✅
- ✅ *"Whaddaya **gonna** do?"* = What **are** you **going to** do?
- ✅ *"Whaddaya **wanna** do?"* = What **do** you **want to** do?

*(Say both, three times each.)* Different helper, different meaning. ⭐

*(Say both versions three times.)* Slow, then fast. Slow, then fast. ✅

*(Point out the pattern.)* Notice which words got crushed: ***do, you, to, about***. All **small grammar words**. ✅

*(THE RULE — this is gold.)*

> ### 👉 English crushes GRAMMAR words and protects MEANING words.

**Crushed** 🗜️: *to, of, for, and, do, you, are, have, a, the, at, can*
**Protected** 🔊: *nouns, main verbs, adjectives, question words*

*(The listening strategy!)* So when you listen to fast English:

**Don't try to catch every word. Catch the LOUD words — they carry most of the meaning.** ✅

*(One caution.)* ⚠️ "Most" is not "all" — the small crushed words carry **grammar**: *don't, didn't, will, has.* ✅ Hunt the loud words first, but keep half an ear on the negatives. ⭐

*(Demo.)* *"...mumble mumble **MEETING** mumble **THREE** mumble **TOMORROW**..."*
→ You got it! **Meeting, three, tomorrow.** ✅ That's the whole message! 🎯

*(Emphasise.)* **Stop trying to hear everything. Hunt for the stressed words.** 🎣

### Sentence 2 — decoded piece by piece

**What you hear:** *"I dunno, lemme check 'n I'll letcha know."*

*(Decode.)*

| You hear | ✅ Actually |
|---|---|
| **dunno** | *don't know* |
| **lemme** | *let me* |
| **'n** | *and* |
| **I'll** | *I will* |
| **letcha** | *let you* |

**Full decode:**
> ✅ **"I don't know, let me check and I'll let you know."** ✅

*(Point out.)* **Eleven words squeezed into about five beats.** No wonder it felt fast! ✅

*(Note the "cha" sound.)* When **t** meets **y**, they merge into **"ch"**:
- *let you* → **letcha** 🔗
- *don't you* → **doncha**
- *what you* → **whatcha**
- *got you* → **gotcha** ⭐
- *nice to meet you* → **nice to meetcha** ✅

*(And when **d** meets **y**, they become "j":)*
- *did you* → **didja** ⭐
- *would you* → **wouldja**
- *could you* → **couldja**
- *had you* → **hadja**

*(Practise.)* **Didja eat? Couldja help? Wouldja mind?** ✅ Say them out loud — you'll hear these constantly in movies. 🎬

### 🔗 THE 3 LINKING RULES

*(Very practical for both listening and speaking.)*

#### Rule 1: Consonant + vowel = they join 🔗

- *an apple* → **"anapple"**
- *pick it up* → **"pickitup"**
- *turn on* → **"turnon"**
- *far away* → **"faraway"**
- *not at all* → **"notatall"** ⭐

*(Practise.)* **"Notatall!"** ✅ That's why you never hear the three separate words. ✅

#### Rule 2: Same consonant twice = one long sound ✂️

- *bus stop* → **"bustop"**
- *black coffee* → **"blacoffee"**
- *hot tea* → **"hotea"**

*(Note.)* ⚠️ *want to → wanna* belongs to a different family — that's a **set phrase** reduction, not this doubling rule. ✅

#### Rule 3: T and D often disappear inside consonant clusters 🕳️

- *next day* → **"nexday"**
- *most people* → **"mospeople"**
- *and then* → **"an'then"**
- *old man* → **"olman"**
- *friendship* → **"frenship"**

*(Point out.)* ⚠️ **"I dunno"** is a bigger squeeze than this rule — the *t* drops **and** the whole phrase collapses into one word. ✅ Learn it as a **set phrase**, not as an example of the T-rule. ⭐

### 👂 THE LISTENING PRACTICE METHOD

*(Extremely practical — give them a real technique.)*

*(Explain.)* Knowing the rules isn't enough. You need **ear training**. Here's the method that works:

**Step 1** — Pick a **30-second** clip *(a movie scene, a YouTube video)* 🎬
**Step 2** — Listen **without** subtitles. Write what you hear. ✍️
**Step 3** — Listen **with** subtitles. Compare.
**Step 4** — Find the reductions you missed. Note them. 📝
**Step 5** — Listen **again** without subtitles. You'll hear them now. ✅

*(Emphasise.)* **Thirty seconds. Not thirty minutes.** ✅ Short and repeated beats long and once. That's how ears train. 👂

*(Best content to practise with.)*
- 🎬 Movies with clear speech *(not action films — too much noise!)*
- 🎙️ Interviews and podcasts *(natural speed, clear audio)*
- 📺 Sitcoms *(short scenes, everyday language)*
- 🏏 Cricket commentary *(you know the context already!)* ⭐

*(Highlight cricket.)* Actually — **cricket commentary is brilliant practice** for Malayalis! You already know what's happening, so your brain can focus purely on the **sounds**. ✅

## ⚠️ 4. THE MALAYALI TRAP (12:00 – 14:00)

### Trap 1: Equal stress on every syllable

*(The core pronunciation difference.)*

*(Explain.)* Because Malayalam gives every syllable **equal weight**, Malayali English often sounds **flat** and **even** — each word pronounced fully and clearly. 🥁

*(Demo the difference.)*

Take the word **"comfortable"** *(4 syllables written)*:
- ⚠️ Malayali habit: **com-for-ta-ble** → all four, equal weight ✅ clear, but very even
- ⭐ Relaxed native speech: **COMF-ter-bl** → squeezed to 3, first one strong! ✅

*(More examples — the stress patterns Malayalis get wrong.)*

| Word | ⚠️ Even | ⭐ Common relaxed pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| comfortable | com-for-ta-ble | **COMF**-ter-bl |
| vegetable | ve-ge-ta-ble | **VEJ**-ta-bl |
| interesting | in-te-res-ting | **IN**-tres-ting |
| temperature | tem-pe-ra-ture | **TEM**-pra-cher |
| chocolate | cho-co-late | **CHOC**-lit |
| business | bu-si-ness | **BIZ**-nis |
| Wednesday | wed-nes-day | **WENZ**-day |
| February | feb-ru-a-ry | **FEB**-yoo-ree |

*(Point out.)* See the pattern? **English often swallows the middle.** 🕳️

*(Fair warning.)* ⚠️ These are the **relaxed** forms, not the only correct ones. ✅ Careful speech keeps more syllables — *COM-for-ta-ble*, *IN-ter-est-ing* — and that's standard too. ✅ Learn these to **hear** fast speech, not because the fuller version is wrong. ⭐

*(Practise these eight out loud — twice each.)*

*(Reassure.)* And note — being **clear** is not a flaw! Indian English clarity is genuinely useful. But knowing the native stress helps you **understand** them. 👂

### Trap 2: Expecting written English in speech

*(A mindset trap.)*

*(Observation.)* Because most Malayalis learned English from **textbooks and exams**, we expect speech to match writing. ✅ So when a native says *"gonna,"* it feels **wrong** — like they made a mistake. 🤔

*(Firmly.)* They didn't. **Written English and spoken English follow different habits.** 📚🗣️

| Written | Spoken |
|---|---|
| I am going to go | **"I'm gonna go"** |
| I do not know | **"I dunno"** |
| Do you want to? | **"Wanna?"** |
| Let us go | **"Let's go"** |

*(Rule.)* **Don't write these in formal English** — emails, essays, exams. ❌ *(You'll see them in novels, subtitles and WhatsApp, where they're perfectly normal.)* ✅ And don't expect writing in speech. ✅

### Trap 3: Speaking too slowly and clearly *(when it hurts you)*

*(A nuanced point.)*

*(Explain.)* Speaking slowly is usually **good** — clarity beats speed. ✅

But there's one thing that genuinely hurts you: **pausing between every word.**

- ❌ *"I ... am ... going ... to ... the ... office."* → sounds like reading 📖
- ✅ *"I'm going to the office."* → sounds like talking ✅

*(The fix — link within phrases.)* Group your words into **chunks** and link inside each chunk:
- ✅ *"I'm-going" ... "to-the-office"* → two chunks ✅

*(Practise chunking.)*
| Sentence | Chunks |
|---|---|
| Could you send me the file? | *"Couldja-send-me"* + *"the-file?"* |
| I'll be there in ten minutes. | *"I'll-be-there"* + *"in-ten-minutes."* |
| Let me know if you need anything. | *"Lemme-know"* + *"if-you-need-anything."* |

*(Emphasise.)* **Chunking is the single fastest way to sound more fluent.** ✅ Not speed. **Grouping.** 🔗

### Trap 4: Copying the accent instead of the rhythm

*(An honest and important correction.)*

*(Firmly, kindly.)* Some learners spend months trying to sound **American**. Please don't. 🚫

*(Explain why.)*
1. It's **very** hard and takes years ⏳
2. It's **completely unnecessary** — nobody at work requires it 💼
3. A faked accent sounds **worse** than a clear natural one 😬
4. Indian English is a **legitimate, respected variety** with 200 million speakers 🇮🇳

*(What actually matters instead.)*

| ✅ Worth learning | ❌ Not worth it |
|---|---|
| **Word stress** *(COMF-ter-bl)* ⭐ | vowel colour *(American "r")* |
| **Chunking and linking** ⭐ | sounding "foreign" |
| **Sentence rhythm** | copying a specific person |
| **Clear consonants** ⭐ | dropping your identity |

*(Emphasise.)* **Rhythm and stress do most of the work for being understood. Copying a native accent is optional.** ✅

*(Warm.)* Speak English **as a Malayali**, clearly and confidently. That's not a compromise — that's **correct**. ❤️
